\begin{abstract}
This project proposes a comparative study of classical numerical
methods, physics-informed neural networks (PINNs), and deep operator
networks (DeepONets) for option valuation and sensitivity analysis.
The study combines controlled Black--Scholes benchmarks with an
empirical case study using Indian equity and derivatives data.
The first stage considers single-asset European options, comparing
Monte Carlo simulation, the Crank--Nicolson finite-difference method,
PINNs, and DeepONets against the analytical solution. Evaluation
will cover pricing and Greek errors, computational cost, training
reliability, repeated valuation, and generalisation across
moneyness, maturity, volatility, and interest rates. Calibration
experiments will examine parameter recovery from synthetic
observations and pricing on held-out market observations.
Model-generated solutions will provide labelled training data
where required, while physics-informed models will use the
governing equation, terminal payoff, and boundary conditions as
training constraints. The second stage considers basket options
on up to five correlated assets, with historical stock returns
informing volatility and correlation inputs. Asset dimension,
correlation structure, and basket weights will also vary.
Monte Carlo with variance reduction and randomised quasi-Monte
Carlo will provide numerical baselines. Analytical geometric
basket values and independent simulation estimates will support
validation. Training, data-generation, and evaluation costs will
be reported separately, and numerical approximation error will
be distinguished from disagreement with observed market prices.
The objective is to identify when neural methods offer useful
computational benefits and when established methods remain
more effective.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:}
Black--Scholes equation; option pricing; physics-informed neural
networks; DeepONet; Monte Carlo; Greeks; basket options;
parameter calibration.

\section{Introduction}

Option pricing connects financial modelling with numerical
computation. An option's value depends on its contractual payoff
and the uncertain evolution of its underlying assets. The
Black--Scholes framework relates these quantities through
no-arbitrage and dynamic hedging
\cite{black1973,merton1973}. Although its assumptions simplify
market behaviour, the availability of analytical solutions and
well-established numerical methods makes it a useful setting
for evaluating alternative computational approaches.

For a European option on a non-dividend-paying asset, the
price \(V(S,t)\) satisfies
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial V}{\partial t}
    + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2
      \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}
    + rS\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}
    - rV = 0,
    \label{eq:bs}
\end{equation}
where \(S\) is the asset price, \(t\) is time, \(r\) is the
risk-free interest rate, and \(\sigma\) is volatility.
For a European call with strike \(K\) and expiry \(T\),
the terminal condition is
\begin{equation}
    V(S,T)=\max(S-K,0).
    \label{eq:call_payoff}
\end{equation}
Under the same assumptions, the option value is the discounted
expected payoff under the risk-neutral probability measure.
The PDE and expectation formulations therefore support
different computational approaches to the same pricing problem.

Finite-difference methods approximate the pricing equation on
a spatial and temporal grid. Monte Carlo methods instead
estimate the expectation from simulated asset values
\cite{glasserman2004}. The analytical Black--Scholes formula
provides an independent reference for single-asset European
options, allowing numerical errors to be measured directly.
An isolated valuation in this setting does not require a neural
approximation. The motivation for investigating neural methods
lies in broader tasks, including sensitivity analysis, repeated
evaluation across related problems, parameter calibration,
and extensions involving several correlated assets.

A physics-informed neural network approximates the option
value by \(V_{\theta}(S,t)\), where \(\theta\) denotes its
trainable parameters. Its loss penalises violations of the
pricing equation, terminal payoff, and boundary conditions
\cite{raissi2019}. Points sampled from the input domain,
known as collocation points, specify where these constraints
are evaluated. They do not require labelled option prices.
Automatic differentiation supplies the derivatives needed
for the PDE residual and the calculation of Greeks.

DeepONets provide an architecture for operator learning.
Their branch and trunk networks encode an input function
and the coordinates of the corresponding output function
\cite{lu2021}. In this study, the architecture will be used
with suitable representations of payoffs and model parameters
to approximate families of price functions. A parameter-conditioned
PINN can also learn across several parameter settings, so
repeated use is not exclusive to DeepONet. Furthermore,
physics-informed constraints can be incorporated into
DeepONet training \cite{wang2021deeponet}. The comparison
will distinguish the network architecture from its training
objective and the information supplied during training.

The role of training data consequently differs between
methods. Classical solvers require model inputs but no
training dataset. A supervised DeepONet requires examples
of pricing problems and their solution values, which can
be generated analytically in one dimension or numerically
for basket options. Physics-informed training uses the
equation and its conditions, with observed prices added
where a data-assisted or calibration experiment requires
them. Data-generation costs will be included whenever
numerical solutions are used as training labels.

The project will combine model-generated benchmarks with
an empirical case study based on Indian market data.
Historical equity prices and option-contract records will
be sourced from NSE or BSE, subject to suitable coverage
and data quality \cite{nseData,bseData}. The initial empirical
study will focus on one exchange and one underlying with
adequate option observations across strikes, maturities,
and dates. Historical stock prices will inform estimates
of return variability, while actual option observations
will support calibration and market-price evaluation.
These data have different roles: a stock-price history
does not itself provide labelled option values.

The market dataset will require consistent records of
underlying prices, option prices, strikes, expiries, and
contract types. Liquidity information will support the
filtering of unreliable observations. Where bid and ask
quotes are available, their spread will help assess
observation quality. End-of-day records will be examined
for possible timing differences between underlying and
option prices. Interest rates, dividends, corporate actions,
and contract specifications will also be considered when
preparing model inputs.

In particular, the non-dividend assumption in
Equation~\eqref{eq:bs} will not be applied to empirical
contracts without examining its suitability. If a constant
continuous dividend yield \(q\) is used, the drift coefficient
in the pricing PDE becomes \(r-q\). The same dividend
treatment and input conventions will be used across the
compared methods.

Controlled benchmarks and market observations answer
different questions. Benchmarks establish whether a
method accurately solves the selected pricing model.
Market observations assess how well that model, together
with its estimated parameters, describes traded prices.
For a numerical estimate \(\widehat{V}\), these differences
can be written as
\begin{align}
    e_{\mathrm{num}}
    &= \widehat{V}-V_{\mathrm{model}},
    \label{eq:numerical_error}\\
    e_{\mathrm{market}}
    &= \widehat{V}-V_{\mathrm{observed}}.
    \label{eq:market_error}
\end{align}
The market discrepancy includes effects beyond numerical
approximation, such as model assumptions, parameter
estimation, and observation quality. A smaller PDE
residual therefore does not necessarily imply closer
agreement with market prices.

Calibration will first be tested using synthetic prices
generated from known parameters. The initial task will
recover volatility while holding the remaining inputs
fixed, followed by experiments with perturbed observations.
The empirical study will then use designated calibration
observations and evaluate prices on held-out contracts
or later dates. Parameter estimation will use only
information available at the relevant evaluation date.
A quote used to infer its own implied volatility will
not also be treated as independent evidence of pricing
accuracy. Historical volatility estimates and option-implied
volatility will be identified separately.

Sensitivity analysis will accompany the pricing comparison.
Delta and Gamma describe the first and second derivatives
with respect to the underlying price, while Vega, Theta,
and Rho measure dependence on volatility, time, and the
interest rate. These quantities may be inaccurate even
when price errors are small. Automatic differentiation
evaluates derivatives of the learned approximation and
does not guarantee agreement with the true sensitivities.
Since market Greeks are not directly observed reference
values, their accuracy will be assessed primarily using
analytical or independently validated numerical benchmarks.

Particular attention will be given to the strike region
near expiry, where the terminal payoff produces sharp
changes in derivatives. The finite-difference implementation
will include appropriate treatment of the payoff and
initial time steps \cite{pooley2003}. Greek evaluation
will distinguish positive time to expiry from the payoff
at expiry, where some derivatives are not defined at
the strike.

For PINNs, controlled experiments will compare uniform
and concentrated collocation sampling, as well as fixed
and adaptive loss weighting. These choices are motivated
by known difficulties in balancing equation and boundary
losses and in optimising PINN approximations
\cite{krishnapriyan2021,wang2021gradients}.
Price errors, Greek errors, and constraint violations
will be measured on points independent of the training
samples. Individual training modifications will be tested
before their combined effect is assessed.

Repeated pricing experiments will investigate whether
the initial cost of a neural model can be recovered
through subsequent evaluations. Training and data generation
will be reported separately from inference, and comparisons
will account for reuse by classical methods, including
interpolation from computed price surfaces. Generalisation
will be measured on parameter combinations excluded from
training, with interpolation within the training range
distinguished from extrapolation beyond it. For models
trained on a fixed parameter setting, the cost of retraining
will be included when the problem changes.

The multidimensional stage considers European basket
options on correlated assets. For \(N\) non-dividend-paying
assets, the pricing equation is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial V}{\partial t}
    + \frac{1}{2}
      \sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}
      \rho_{ij}\sigma_i\sigma_j S_iS_j
      \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S_i\partial S_j}
    + r\sum_{i=1}^{N}S_i\frac{\partial V}{\partial S_i}
    - rV = 0,
    \label{eq:multi_bs}
\end{equation}
where \(\sigma_i\) is the volatility of asset \(i\), and
\(\rho_{ij}\) is the correlation between the Brownian
drivers of assets \(i\) and \(j\). An arithmetic basket
call has payoff
\begin{equation}
    V(\mathbf{S},T)
    =
    \max\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_iS_i(T)-K,0\right),
    \qquad
    w_i\geq0,\qquad
    \sum_{i=1}^{N}w_i=1.
    \label{eq:basket_payoff}
\end{equation}
Dividend adjustments will be incorporated consistently
when required by the empirical inputs.

The principal basket case will use five stocks, with
two- and three-asset cases providing intermediate
comparisons. Aligned, corporate-action-adjusted return
histories will inform volatility and correlation estimates.
These estimates provide empirically motivated scenarios;
they will not be presented as automatically calibrated
risk-neutral parameters. Unless observations for the exact
basket contract are available, this experiment represents
a hypothetical derivative constructed from real underlying
assets rather than a direct validation against a traded
basket-option price.

Classical multidimensional pricing methods exist, but
a full grid with \(m\) points per asset contains \(m^N\)
spatial points. This limits direct extensions of the
one-dimensional solver. Monte Carlo with variance reduction
and randomised quasi-Monte Carlo will therefore provide
the principal basket baselines. Analytical geometric
basket values will support validation and control-variate
construction, while arithmetic basket references will
be computed independently with statistical uncertainty
reported \cite{glasserman2004}. Reference sensitivities
will also require separate error checks.

Neural methods avoid explicitly constructing a full
spatial grid, but this does not remove all dimensional
difficulties. Sampling, training, and evaluating the mixed
derivatives in Equation~\eqref{eq:multi_bs} may still
become expensive. The experiments will therefore report
performance over the tested dimensions without assuming
that results at five assets establish general
high-dimensional scalability.

The financial inputs will vary across moneyness \(S/K\),
time to maturity \(\tau=T-t\), volatility \(\sigma\),
and the risk-free interest rate \(r\). Single-asset tests
will cover European calls and puts in out-of-the-money,
at-the-money, and in-the-money regions. Parameter ranges
for the controlled benchmarks will be specified before
evaluation and informed by the market-data preparation
stage. In basket experiments, the number of assets,
individual volatilities, correlation structure, and
basket weights will also vary. All correlation matrices
will be constrained to be valid.

Performance will be assessed using root-mean-square
and maximum absolute pricing errors, together with
errors in Delta, Gamma, Vega, Theta, and Rho.
The basket study will prioritise asset-wise Delta,
diagonal Gamma, and selected cross-Gamma terms.
Relative errors will be reported only where reference
values are sufficiently far from zero. Separate
measurements will cover training and data-generation
time, price and Greek evaluation time, memory use,
and total cost for repeated queries. Calibration will
be evaluated through parameter recovery, held-out
pricing error, and sensitivity to observation noise.

Numerical and training settings will be varied
separately from financial inputs. These include
grid resolution for Crank--Nicolson, sample count
for Monte Carlo, and collocation count, sampling
strategy, loss weighting, and training budget for
the neural methods. Controlled changes to these
settings will establish convergence behaviour and
help identify the source of observed improvements.
Repeated training runs will measure variability,
and financial consistency checks will examine
applicable price bounds, put--call parity,
monotonicity, and convexity.

The intended contribution is a reproducible comparison
that connects controlled numerical validation with
an Indian-market application. Its conclusions will
address when neural methods justify their training
costs, how reliably they produce sensitivities and
calibrated prices, and how their performance changes
across the tested asset dimensions. The study will
report both successful approximations and observed
limitations, with comparisons made at similar accuracy
wherever possible.
